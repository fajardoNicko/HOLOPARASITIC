**Citation** | Price, C. A., & Weitz, J. S. (2014). Costs and benefits of reticulate leaf venation. *BMC Plant Biology, 14*, 234.
**DOI** | 10.1186/s12870-014-0234-2
**Access level** | FULL TEXT READ (all sections incl. Methods)
**Study type** | Empirical network extraction + computational damage simulation
**System studied** | 339 cleared whole-leaf images, 324 species, 72–74 eudicot families (Smithsonian National Cleared Leaf Collection), 3,934,626 vein segments, extracted with LEAF GUI
**Main finding** | (i) They plot *fraction of network still connected to the petiole vs. fraction of vein segments removed* (Fig. 1) — an explicit percolation curve — for real reticulate networks vs. their Maximum Spanning Tree (MST) analogues. "When 10% of the vein segments are removed in a hierarchical tree, essentially all nodes are disconnected from the petiole, while approximately 50% of the nodes in a reticulate network remain connected." (ii) Robustness rises with VLA, loopiness and meshedness, **with meshedness the best predictor** (Results; Fig. 2; Additional file 2 Table S2). (iii) Descriptive stats: VLA 1.37–10.76 mm⁻¹ (mean 3.17); loopiness 0.19–5.20 areoles mm⁻²(mean 1.40); **meshedness M = (m−n+1)/(2n−5), range 0.06–0.26, mean 0.14, SD 0.04**; robustness 0.026–0.150. (iv) Costs of reticulation vs. MST are small: length +6.3%, width +12%, surface area +5.6%, volume +3.3%, **theoretical conductance +0.51%**; cost approaches ~5% by volume for the loopiest/densest leaves. (v) Distance from non-vein tissue to network is statistically indistinguishable between reticulate nets and MSTs (p > 0.05).
**Relevant variable** | Loop fraction / meshedness; mean degree ⟨k⟩; p_c; the whole random-removal percolation curve; construction cost
**Exact relevance** | This is the closest published antecedent to the entire paper and must be engaged with directly. It independently establishes the paper's core causal claim — that topological redundancy indices predict damage robustness in real leaf venation — on 339 leaves. Their meshedness is an affine transform of the paper's loop fraction for planar graphs, so the paper's loop-fraction ↔ targeted p_c ρ = +0.818 and bridge fraction ↔ p_c ρ = −0.818 are the site-percolation analogue of Price & Weitz's meshedness ↔ robustness result. Their conductance cost of +0.51% is the number the paper needs when defending the reticulation-enhancement experiment (median Δp_c +0.0095, 93/105 improved): raising ⟨k⟩ to the cohort P90 is cheap in hydraulic terms. Critically, they run **bond (edge) percolation**; this paper runs **site (node) percolation** — that difference must be stated, not glossed.
**Paragraph location** | RRL (primary anchor); Methods (justifying redundancy indices); Results-interpretation (loop-fraction correlations); Discussion-cost/benefit of reticulation; Limitations (bond vs site)
**Claim it can support** | Across 339 angiosperm leaves, structural redundancy indices — especially meshedness — positively predict the fraction of the network that remains connected to the petiole under simulated random vein removal, while the material and conductance costs of that redundancy are minor (≤6.3% length, 0.51% conductance).
**Strength** | Essential
**Caveat** | Bond not site percolation; unmagnified, lower-resolution whole-leaf images (their VLA is admittedly low); "robustness" is defined relative to an MST counterpart, not as a critical threshold, so it yields **no p_c value comparable to 0.200**; random removal only — no targeted attack; and they explicitly concede that the Hagen–Poiseuille step assumes "a consistent proportionality between vessel bundle dimensions and the dimensions of the xylem vessels they contain," stating "it is not currently known if a constant proportionality exists between vein diameters and xylem diameters."

**Citation** | Katifori, E., Szöllősi, G. J., & Magnasco, M. O. (2010). Damage and fluctuations induce loops in optimal transport networks. *Physical Review Letters, 104*(4), 048704.
**DOI** | 10.1103/PhysRevLett.104.048704
**Access level** | FULL TEXT READ (arXiv:0906.0006, identical to published Letter)
**Study type** | Theory / numerical optimization + a small physical experiment
**System studied** | Resistor-network model on a planar graph (N = 285, 949, 1589 nodes); lemon (*Citrus*) leaf dye-injection experiment
**Main finding** | Two independent functionals both produce loops at the optimum. **Damage model:** R = Σ_(ab) P_ab, the total dissipation summed over every single-bond cut; finiteness of R requires that no single bond disconnect the graph, but the optimum contains *far more* loops than the minimum topologically required (a perimeter ring). **Fluctuating-load model:** F = Σ_a P_a with a single moving sink; produces "dense, recursively-looped structures." Cost of a conductance C is C^γ; the classic tree/sheet transition sits at γ = 1. The cut-bond model keeps a cusp at γ = 1 like the uniform-load case, but **the moving-sink model replaces the phase transition with a crossover** (Fig. 3 inset). Footnote 18: "for a pipe network with Poiseuille flow γ = 0.25 whereas for a resistor network... γ = 1." Fig. 1 (empirical): a circular cut was made through the main vein of a lemon leaf and fluorescein injected at the stem; dye "was observed to flow through the vein network around the injury, closing a number of loops and eventually reaching the tip of the leaf," with the two secondary veins immediately downstream **carrying reversed flow** from tertiary veins back into the primary vein.
**Relevant variable** | Loop fraction; why loops exist at all; the damage functional; Hagen–Poiseuille γ
**Exact relevance** | Supplies the theoretical warrant for treating loop fraction as a *functional* rather than merely descriptive trait, and — via Fig. 1 — supplies the only direct experimental demonstration in this literature that a real leaf reroutes flow around a severed vein. That image is the physical event the paper's percolation model abstracts. The γ = 0.25 footnote also matters: the paper's Hagen–Poiseuille resistor network sits in the regime where optimal networks are *hierarchical trees*, not loopy meshes, under stationary load — so the observed median loop fraction of 0.776 is itself evidence that stationary-load optimization is not what shaped these networks.
**Paragraph location** | Intro (why loops exist); RRL; Discussion-mechanism (rerouting around damage); Discussion-interpretation of loop fraction
**Claim it can support** | Optimal transport networks are loopless trees only under stationary, undamaged load; introducing either random link damage or a fluctuating sink into the optimization makes recursively nested loops optimal, and dye tracing confirms that real leaf venation reroutes flow around a severed primary vein.
**Strength** | Essential
**Caveat** | Optimization theory, not a robustness measurement: it explains why loops are *selected for*, not how much damage a given real network tolerates. No percolation threshold is computed, no empirical network is analysed, and the lemon-leaf experiment is a single qualitative demonstration (n = 1, no statistics). Their damage model deletes **bonds**, not nodes.

**Citation** | Corson, F. (2010). Fluctuations and redundancy in optimal transport networks. *Physical Review Letters, 104*(4), 048703.
**DOI** | 10.1103/PhysRevLett.104.048703
**Access level** | FULL TEXT READ (arXiv:0905.4947)
**Study type** | Theory / numerical optimization
**System studied** | n×n square resistor lattices (n = 8, 12, 16), N−1 sources + one corner sink, fluctuation amplitude σ
**Main finding** | Minimizing ⟨J⟩ = ⟨Σ I²_kl/κ_kl⟩ subject to Σκ^γ = K^γ. Under stationary flow, all local minima are spanning trees for γ < 1 (Refs. Banavar 2000, Bohn & Magnasco 2007). With fluctuating sources, **loops appear above a finite threshold γ_c** and increase to a maximum as γ → 1 (Fig. 2a), giving "hierarchical networks with loops" at intermediate γ ≈ 0.75 (Fig. 1b) — distinct from both the tree (γ = 0.25) and the uniform loopy sheet (γ = 1.25). He introduces an **entropy-based redundancy measure** R (Eqs. 10–13), the path-distribution entropy averaged between each source and the sink, where R = 0 for a single path and log n for n independent equal-conductance paths; R tracks loop number but avoids the arbitrary conductance threshold. γ_c is larger (transition sharper) for smaller fluctuation amplitude (Fig. 3). Loop number scales as (n−1)², implying constant average loop area; γ_c appears size-independent. He argues leaf veins are bundles of vessels behaving like porous media, so γ is "close to 1 (yet smaller than 1)" — "the range where the structure of optimal networks is most sensitive to fluctuations."
**Relevant variable** | Loop fraction; redundancy metrics; finite-size behaviour
**Exact relevance** | Two specific uses. (1) Corson's explicit critique — "the number of loops... is not an ideal measure of network redundancy, as it is a purely topological one, and depends on an arbitrary threshold" — is the strongest published statement the paper can cite when justifying why it reports *both* loop fraction and ⟨k⟩ and D rather than a single index, and when it hedges the headroom diagnostic. (2) His finite-size analysis (Fig. 4: L/(n−1)² and R/n at n = 8, 12, 16) is a precedent for exactly the kind of size-dependence check the paper ran and then declined to make claims from (p_c falling 0.2701 → 0.0772 as N goes 1023 → 16383): Corson found loop number scale-invariant but *normalized redundancy increasing less rapidly above γ_c for larger n*, i.e. redundancy metrics are not size-neutral. That is direct support for the decision to make **no finite-size claim**.
**Paragraph location** | Intro/RRL; Methods (choice of redundancy metric); Limitations (finite size; topological metrics are threshold-dependent)
**Claim it can support** | Purely topological loop counts are threshold-dependent and imperfect measures of network redundancy, and normalized redundancy measures do not scale neutrally with system size — motivating multiple complementary descriptors and caution about extrapolating threshold values across network sizes.
**Strength** | Essential
**Caveat** | Regular square lattices with uncorrelated fluctuations only; no empirical leaf data; γ for real leaf veins is asserted, not measured. Establishes nothing about node-removal thresholds.

**Citation** | Sack, L., Dietrich, E. M., Streeter, C. M., Sánchez-Gómez, D., & Holbrook, N. M. (2008). Leaf palmate venation and vascular redundancy confer tolerance of hydraulic disruption. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 105*(5), 1567–1572.
**DOI** | 10.1073/pnas.0709333105
**Access level** | FULL TEXT READ (main text; SI figures referenced but not opened)
**Study type** | In vivo severing experiment on intact plants
**System studied** | Woody angiosperms of contrasting venation architecture — pinnate (*Quercus rubra*, *Kalmia latifolia*, *Viburnum cassinoides*) vs. palmate (*Acer rubrum*, *A. saccharum*, *Viburnum acerifolium*); 8 species across treatments
**Main finding** | Measurements taken **2–9 weeks after wounds healed**. Severing the midrib in pinnate leaves reduced stomatal conductance *g* by 46% ± 4.4% (*K. latifolia*) to 94% ± 1.1% (*Q. rubra*); K_leaf fell 62% ± 7.9% to 95% ± 1%; on average across the three pinnate species K_leaf and E_leaf fell **75% ± 10% and 63% ± 14%**, and ΔΨ_leaf rose 66% ± 19%. Palmate leaves showed *no* overall impact on K_leaf or ΔΨ_leaf, and *g* fell only 10% ± 8.1% (proximal) and 20% ± 4.1% (distal). Tolerance correlated with **1° vein density** (r_s = 0.79–0.96) but **not** with 2° or combined 1°+2° vein density in the intact leaf (r_s = 0.14–0.39, P = 0.38–0.76). Crucially: severing a 2° vein had **no impact** on *g*, Φ_PSII, E_leaf, ΔΨ_leaf or K_leaf in any species; punching 5 or 10 holes (severing 3° and minor veins) had **no significant impact** on any parameter in the eight species tested, with Φ_PSII reduced only 2.9% ± 0.8% on the hole itself. Mortality 0–6%.
**Relevant variable** | Loop fraction / reticulation as damage tolerance; targeted (hub) vs. random removal
**Exact relevance** | The empirical keystone. "All leaves tested showed complete tolerance of damage to second- and higher-order veins, demonstrating that the parallel flow paths provided by the redundant, reticulate minor vein network protect the leaf from the impact of hydraulic disruption." That is the physiological cash value of a median loop fraction of 0.776 and a median GCC fraction of 1.000. It also independently validates the paper's random-vs-targeted contrast from the *biological* side: removing a random minor-vein junction is functionally free, while removing the single highest-degree element (the midrib) costs 75% of K_leaf in a pinnate leaf. That asymmetry is the physiological reading of p_c 0.200 (random) vs 0.169 (degree-targeted) and the 15.7% reduction.
**Paragraph location** | Intro (motivation); RRL; Results-interpretation (random vs degree-targeted gap); Discussion-biological meaning of loop fraction
**Claim it can support** | In vivo severing shows that angiosperm leaves are completely tolerant of damage to second- and higher-order veins because the reticulate minor-vein network supplies parallel flow paths, whereas severing the single first-order vein in a pinnately veined leaf reduces leaf hydraulic conductance by roughly three-quarters.
**Strength** | Essential
**Caveat** | Small species set (3 pinnate, 3 palmate for the midrib treatment); measurements *after wound healing*, so it reports acclimated not instantaneous response; tolerance of *major* damage was predicted by **1° vein density, an architectural not a reticulation trait** — the reticulate minor network buffered minor damage but was explicitly *not* what predicted midrib tolerance. Do not cite this as showing minor-vein reticulation predicts tolerance of major disruption; it shows the opposite. Also: damage here is vein *severing* (edge cut), not junction deletion.

**Citation** | Ronellenfitsch, H., Lasser, J., Daly, D. C., & Katifori, E. (2015). Topological phenotypes constitute a new dimension in the phenotypic space of leaf venation networks. *PLoS Computational Biology, 11*(12), e1004680.
**DOI** | 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004680
**Access level** | FULL TEXT READ (arXiv:1507.04487v3; main text + Methods)
**Study type** | Empirical topological phenotyping + developmental model
**System studied** | 186 leaves and leaflets, 137 species, predominantly Burseraceae (*Protium* n = 98, *Bursera* n = 21, *Parkia* n = 8); chemically cleared, scanned at 6400 dpi, vectorized in house
**Main finding** | Hierarchical loop decomposition maps the network to a binary nesting tree; nesting ratio q_j = s_j/r_j; unweighted and degree-weighted nesting numbers plus mean topological length L_top. Five *geometric* quantities were computed for comparison (vein density σ, mean inter-vein distance, mean areole area, areole density, mean vein diameter). **PCA: first two components explain 81% (52% + 29%) of total variance; Component 1 is mostly geometry, Component 2 mostly topology** — "local geometrical and topological leaf traits [are] approximately orthogonal traits for the description of the phenotype of leaf venation." Leaves were fragmented *in silico* into **~1.2 × 1.2 cm segments**; LDA classification accuracy to specimen was **0.35 (95% CI 0.31–0.39) using geometry alone, rising to 0.54 (95% CI 0.48–0.60) when topology was added**. The metric "depends not on exact measurements of vein diameters but only on relative order," and node-position perturbations do not affect the outcome (Fig. 1h). Nesting statistics were reproduced by a growth model with four dimensionless parameters (ρ, β, α, f_n) in which per-species developmental noise sets nestedness.
**Relevant variable** | Loop fraction and ⟨k⟩ as topological traits distinct from D and vein density; crop-window validity; tracing-quality robustness
**Exact relevance** | Three separate loads. (1) It is the citable authority that **topology carries information orthogonal to geometry** — the licence for the paper's claim that ⟨k⟩ and loop fraction are not redundant with D or VLA, and it dovetails with the observed weak collinearity between ⟨k⟩ and D (rank ρ = 0.22) and the partial correlations (⟨k⟩|D 0.736; D|⟨k⟩ 0.503). (2) It is the **strongest available defence of the Matos crop-window limitation**: this group deliberately computed venation fingerprints on 1.2 × 1.2 cm fragments and found topology *improved* fragment identification by 19 percentage points. Local topological metrics are designed to be estimable "from leaf segments alone if necessary." (3) Its robustness-to-relative-order-only claim supports the paper's finding that reticulation gain was similar across tracing-quality grades A–D (Δ = 0.0069/0.0137/0.0083/0.0086).
**Paragraph location** | Intro; RRL; Methods (metric choice); Results-interpretation (⟨k⟩ vs D collinearity); **Limitations (crop windows — this is the key citation)**
**Claim it can support** | Topological descriptors of leaf venation encode information approximately orthogonal to geometric descriptors such as vein density, and are explicitly designed to be computed from centimetre-scale leaf fragments, where adding topology raises fragment identification accuracy from 0.35 to 0.54.
**Strength** | Essential
**Caveat** | Taxonomically narrow (one family dominates); their topological metric is *nesting*, not mean degree or bridge fraction, so orthogonality is demonstrated for their metric and does not automatically transfer to ⟨k⟩; whole scanned leaves were the source data and fragments were cut *in silico* from complete networks — which is **not** the same as the Matos situation, where the crop window is all that was ever imaged and no whole-blade context exists. Establishes nothing about hydraulics or robustness.

**Citation** | Ronellenfitsch, H., & Katifori, E. (2019). Phenotypes of vascular flow networks. *Physical Review Letters, 123*(24), 248101.
**DOI** | 10.1103/PhysRevLett.123.248101
**Access level** | FULL TEXT READ (arXiv:1707.03074v3)
**Study type** | Theory / adaptive-dynamics simulation + Pareto analysis
**System studied** | Disordered planar mesh, 445 nodes / 1255 edges, adaptation dK_e/dt = ⟨F²_e⟩^β − K_e + κe^(−t/ρ), α = 1, β = 2/3, γ = 1/2, spatially correlated Gaussian load fluctuations of scale σ ∈ [0.1, 5]
**Main finding** | Three objectives are minimized simultaneously: power dissipation E, cost C (γ = 1/2 ≡ total vessel volume), and a **"percolation penalty" A = (1/N_e)Σ_e A_e/A_tot — the expected fraction of perfused area disconnected from the source upon removing an edge**. The steady states form a dense cloud; the Pareto front is approximately one-dimensional (**PCA1 encodes 91% of variance**) and is parametrized by the fluctuation length scale σ alone — ρ and κ do not parametrize it. Endpoints are functional archetypes: σ ≫ L̂ gives "low-cost, fragile and non-reticulate, high dissipation networks (tree archetype)"; σ ≪ L̂ gives "high-cost, robust and fully reticulate, low dissipation networks (reticulate archetype)." **The Pareto front comprises 14% of all networks**; mean distance from the front ⟨d_P⟩ ≈ 0.24; of the non-Pareto remainder 70% lie closer than average and 30% further. Off-front phenotypes show less hierarchical organization for the same reticulation, resembling *Ginkgo biloba*.
**Relevant variable** | Loop fraction; p_c; the cost side of the reticulation-enhancement experiment
**Exact relevance** | Two things. (1) A published biological-network study that uses a **connectivity-loss-under-edge-removal penalty as an explicit optimization objective** — the paper's percolation lens is not an idiosyncratic import from statistical physics but an established objective in the venation-optimization literature. (2) It is the necessary corrective to the reticulation-enhancement result. Raising every leaf's ⟨k⟩ to the cohort P90 of 2.811 moves it along an axis that Ronellenfitsch & Katifori show is a **three-way trade-off**: robustness is bought with cost and, at the tree end, with dissipation. The paper's Δp_c = +0.0095 with 0/105 worsened should be reported alongside the fact that the enhancement is not free in a Pareto sense, and Price & Weitz 2014 supplies the empirical magnitude of that price (+3.3% volume, +0.51% conductance).
**Paragraph location** | RRL; Discussion-reticulation enhancement (the cost caveat); Discussion-why real leaves are not maximally reticulate; Limitations
**Claim it can support** | Venation architecture can be understood as occupying a one-dimensional Pareto front trading transport efficiency, construction cost and damage robustness against one another, tuned by a single developmental parameter — so an intervention that raises robustness necessarily moves a network along that front rather than strictly improving it.
**Strength** | Essential
**Caveat** | **The manuscript's reference list gives this as PRL 123:038301 — that article number is wrong.** The correct locator is *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 123(24), 248101 (12 Dec 2019), DOI 10.1103/PhysRevLett.123.248101, verified via Crossref. (038301 does not resolve to this paper; the likely source of the confusion is the same authors' PRL 117, 138301 (2016), "Global optimization, local adaptation, and the role of growth in distribution networks.") Substantively: a 445-node simulation with no empirical leaf data; the percolation penalty is an *edge*-removal expectation, not a critical threshold, so it yields no number comparable to p_c = 0.200.

**Citation** | Mileyko, Y., Edelsbrunner, H., Price, C. A., & Weitz, J. S. (2012). Hierarchical ordering of reticular networks. *PLoS ONE, 7*(6), e36715.

**Citation** | Katifori, E., & Magnasco, M. O. (2012). Quantifying loopy network architectures. *PLoS ONE, 7*(6), e37994.
**DOI** | 10.1371/journal.pone.0037994 | **Access** | FULL TEXT READ (pp. 1–3 of arXiv:1110.1412; later sections skimmed) | **Type** | Methods / comparative network analysis
**System** | Computer-generated graphs, optimal distribution networks, digitized dicot leaves, rat cerebral neocortex vasculature
**Main finding** | Presents the **hierarchical loop decomposition** mapping loopy planar networks to binary trees, quantified by Asymmetry, cumulative size distribution and Strahler bifurcation ratios. The stated motivation is that existing tools fail: Horton–Strahler and Asymmetry "presuppose a tree architecture and loops destroy their consistency," while generic complex-network measures such as degree distribution "are not fine tuned to capture many aspects of the 2-d network organization." They argue explicitly for metrics that "do not rely on the exact value of the bond strength or geometrical location," enabling "phenotypic parameter reduction and assignment of numeric values to the level of loop nestedness... not represented by descriptions that rely on local, scalar quantities (such as histograms of the vein density)." ## BRIDGE VALUE

**Price & Weitz (2014) — the load-bearing bridge.** Uniquely spans all four components at once: real traced venation → graph topology (meshedness, loopiness) → simulated damage/percolation curve → Hagen–Poiseuille conductance cost. *Argument enabled:* the paper is not importing percolation into plant biology; it is extending an existing plant-biology result from bond to site percolation, from random to targeted attack, and from a relative robustness index to a critical threshold. This reframes the contribution from "novel application" (weak, and vulnerable) to "methodological extension with a specific increment" (defensible). It also, in its own words, hands over the Hagen–Poiseuille caveat about vein-vs-conduit diameter proportionality, letting the paper cite a limitation to the very source that shares it.

**Katifori et al. (2010) + Sack et al. (2008) — theory ↔ experiment.** Katifori's damage functional predicts loops; Sack's severing experiment measures what loops buy (complete tolerance of ≥2° vein damage; 75% K_leaf loss without them). *Argument enabled:* loop fraction has both a derivation and a measurement behind it, so the paper's loop-fraction correlations are anchored at both ends rather than being a graph statistic in search of meaning. Katifori's lemon-leaf fluorescein image supplies the physical picture; Sack supplies the numbers.

**Brodribb et al. (2016) + Scoffoni et al. (2017) + Lu et al. (2025) — the failure-mode bridge.** Together they specify, from three independent methods, *how leaf vascular networks actually fail*: initiation in the largest vein and the petiole/midrib, ordering set by conduit size, propagation spatially contagious, more connected networks showing a more ordered thick-to-thin sequence. *Argument enabled:* a principled and quantitative account of the gap between the model's random site percolation and real hydraulic failure — converting the paper's biggest vulnerability into an explicitly bounded modelling choice, justifying the degree-targeted mode as the more physically faithful of the two, and naming correlated-removal percolation as the concrete next step. Combined with Sack et al. (2008), it also motivates keeping *both* attack modes: mechanical/herbivory damage is approximately random and well tolerated; hydraulic failure is hub-first and is not.

**Ronellenfitsch et al. (2015) + Green et al. (2014) + Blonder et al. (2020) — the crop-window bridge.** Three independent lines: topological metrics were purpose-designed for ~1.2 cm fragments and improve fragment ID from 0.35 to 0.54; modal areole size is consistent across large sections of lamina (120 leaves, 80 families); 260-species venation phenotyping was done on ≤2 cm² samples in *New Phytologist*. *Argument enabled:* the Matos crop windows are a recognized sampling regime for local venation topology, not a fatal defect — while Blonder's scale-dependence finding and Scoffoni et al. (2017)'s petiole/midrib result honestly bound what the windows can support (local topology yes; global connectivity metrics such as GCC and p_c, and anything about the petiole, no).

**Ronellenfitsch & Katifori (2019) + Nardini et al. (2012) + Price & Weitz (2014) — the cost bridge.** Formal Pareto structure (efficiency/cost/percolation-penalty, 1-D front, 14% of networks) + ecological price (higher LMA, smaller leaves, reduced competitiveness) + measured material price (+3.3% volume, +0.51% conductance). *Argument enabled:* a complete, quantified answer to "if reticulation improves p_c and 0 of 105 leaves got worse, why aren't all leaves maximally reticulate?" — namely that the model optimizes one axis of a genuine three-way trade-off, and the missing axes have known signs and approximate magnitudes.

**Blonder et al. (2018) + Blonder et al. (2020) — the interpretive bridge.** Reticulation's ecological signal runs to mechanical strength and chemical defence; topology–function linkages are weak and scale-dependent. *Argument enabled:* the paper's most defensible thesis statement. Reticulation *does* confer measurable topological robustness (Price & Weitz; Sack et al.; this paper), but robustness is plausibly a by-product of selection acting on defence, support and cost. Stated this way the paper is consistent with all the counterevidence rather than exposed to it, and the headroom diagnostic (predicted 0.2595 vs measured 0.1998; predicted gain 3.7× the rewired gain) becomes evidence *for* this interpretation rather than an embarrassment: leaves are not sitting where a robustness-only optimizer would put them.
