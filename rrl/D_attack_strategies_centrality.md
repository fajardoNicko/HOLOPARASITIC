## ANSWERS TO THE FOUR QUESTIONS

### Q1 — Defensible framing for centrality-targeted removal when no agent computes centrality

There are **three independent legitimating moves** in the literature, and the paper should use all three, in this order.

**(a) The envelope / bracketing move (formal, physics).** Trajanovski et al. (2013) is explicit that random removal characterises *expected-case* behaviour while centrality-targeted removal approximates the *extrema* of the response distribution, and that the pair together bounds the space of realisations. Iyer et al. (2013) concur that centrality-targeted attacks give a good indication of a network's worst-case behaviour. Neither framing requires an agent.

> *Defensible sentence form:* "Following the robustness-envelope framework of Trajanovski et al. (2013), we treat random and centrality-ordered removal not as competing models of parasitic damage but as a bracket: random removal characterises the expected structural response of the network, and degree- and betweenness-ordered removal approximate its pessimistic extreme. No claim is made that any organism computes or acts upon a centrality measure."

> *Second form, for the Limitations paragraph:* "A haustorium plainly does not rank vein junctions by betweenness. The targeted modes are therefore reported as structural stress tests that bound how much worse an equivalent loss of vascular nodes could be if the lost nodes were structurally non-random, and not as a model of parasite behaviour."

**(b) The biological-precedent move.** Memmott et al. (2004) removed pollinators in strict linkage order in *Proc R Soc B* despite no extinction process being linkage-ordered, precisely because the extreme orderings bracket the plausible range. This move is two decades old and standard in ecological network science.

> *Defensible sentence form:* "Connectivity-ordered removal sequences have been used as bracketing scenarios in biological network analysis since Memmott et al. (2004), where pollinators were removed in strict order of linkage although no real extinction process is so ordered; the ordering is a device for bounding outcomes, not a hypothesis about mechanism."

**(c) The load-proxy move — the strongest one, and the one that converts a weakness into a result.** This is the move I would prioritise. Motter and Lai (2002) operationalise a node's *load* in a flow network as its betweenness centrality. Your networks are not abstract graphs: they are Hagen–Poiseuille resistor networks with an explicit Kirchhoff solve, so every junction carries a real flux. Betweenness is the topological proxy for that flux. A betweenness-ordered removal is therefore identical in ordering to a **highest-flux-first failure sequence** — which is a mechanistically plausible failure ordering under sink-driven tension, since the highest-flux conduits experience the greatest tension and are the most embolism-prone.

> *Defensible sentence form:* "Betweenness centrality is the standard topological proxy for the load carried by a node in a flow network (Motter & Lai, 2002). In a hydraulic network, betweenness-ordered removal therefore corresponds to a highest-flux-first failure sequence — the ordering expected if failure probability increases with the tension a conduit sustains — rather than to deliberate targeting. Degree-ordered removal has no comparable hydraulic interpretation and is reported purely as a structural reference."

**Strong recommendation:** the paper should compute the rank correlation between node betweenness and the node flux from its own Kirchhoff solve. If that correlation is high, move (c) stops being an argument by analogy and becomes a measured result in your own data, and the entire vulnerability evaporates. This is a few lines of code on data you already have and is, in my judgement, the highest-value single addition available to this paper.

**Sentence forms to avoid:** anything of the form "the parasite preferentially attacks high-betweenness junctions," "targeted attack simulates haustorial site selection," or "the parasite exploits network hubs." Nothing in your data or in this literature supports any of these.

### Q2 — Why betweenness beats degree here, and does the network type predict it?

**Yes — the ordering is predicted, and it is predicted specifically by the planarity and degree-homogeneity of your networks.** This is a genuine mechanistic result, not a curiosity.

**The mechanism, in three steps:**

1. **Degree is bounded and near-degenerate in planar graphs.** Mou and Hayashi (2025) state that in planar graphs node degrees are generally bounded and do not exceed about six, with bell-shaped distributions peaking at k = 2–4, and report a uniform-RNG mean degree of ≈2.50 — statistically indistinguishable from your median ⟨k⟩ of 2.520. With SD 0.848 around a mean of 2.520, a venation graph is essentially a mixture of degree-1 tips and degree-3 branch points. **A degree ranking on such a network is almost entirely ties.** If ≈76% of nodes share the modal degree, then "remove the top 20% by degree" is in practice "remove an arbitrary tie-broken quarter of the degree-3 nodes" — which is close to a random removal restricted to non-tip nodes. This is why degree targeting buys you only 15.7% (0.200 → 0.169) on the empirical cohort: **there is almost no information in the degree ranking to exploit.**

2. **Betweenness is continuous and identifies the backbone.** Betweenness has no such ceiling; in a sparse planar network it concentrates sharply on the small set of segments carrying flow between regions. Mou and Hayashi (2025) establish for planar proximity graphs the ordering q_c^RB < q_c^ID < q_c^RF — betweenness most damaging, degree intermediate, random least — the same ordering as your synthetic reference (0.1889 < 0.2023 < 0.271). Al Musawi et al. (2023) independently find betweenness attacks especially destructive because they remove the nodes controlling paths between network sections. Wandelt et al. (2018) find iterative betweenness the best of thirteen dismantling methods in 70–80% of cases.

3. **Sparse near-tree-like networks are rich in articulation points, and betweenness finds them.** Tian et al. (2017) show that articulation-point density is unimodal in mean degree, peaking at ⟨k⟩ ≈ 1.42 for ER, with tree-like and sparse networks containing substantially more articulation points than dense ones, and that AP-targeted removal is highly efficient — outperforming degree-based strategies on many real networks. At ⟨k⟩ = 2.520 with 22.4% bridge edges, your leaves are firmly in the AP-rich regime. High-betweenness nodes in such a network *are* largely articulation points and bridge endpoints. **This is precisely why your bridge fraction correlates −0.818 with targeted p_c and loop fraction +0.818** — the same structural quantity that makes a leaf bridge-rich makes it betweenness-attackable.

> *Defensible sentence form:* "The ordering we observe — betweenness more damaging than degree — is the reverse of the ordering commonly reported for scale-free networks, but it is the ordering expected for our network class. In planar graphs node degree is bounded and narrowly distributed (Mou & Hayashi, 2025), so a degree ranking is largely degenerate and conveys little targeting information; betweenness, by contrast, is continuously valued and concentrates on the sparse backbone and articulation points that are abundant in sparse, near-tree-like graphs (Tian et al., 2017). The correspondence between this and our bridge-fraction correlation (r = −0.818 with the targeted threshold) indicates that the betweenness attack is, structurally, a bridge attack."

**Actionable addition:** report the **number of nodes tied at the cut degree** in your degree-targeted attack, and state your tie-breaking rule. A reviewer who works this out independently will conclude the degree mode is barely a targeted attack. Reporting it yourself turns the same fact into your central mechanistic argument. Also report the SD or Gini of betweenness alongside the degree SD of 0.848 — the contrast between a near-degenerate degree distribution and a heavy-tailed betweenness distribution *is* the explanation, and it is one number.

### Q3 — Initial vs recalculated centrality: what to concede

**Concede clearly, quantify honestly, and then extract the two advantages that static ranking genuinely gives you.**

**The concession.** Holme et al. (2002) established from the outset that recalculated degree and betweenness removals are often more harmful than initial-ranking removals. Wandelt et al. (2018) put a magnitude on it: iterative betweenness (BI) was the top performer in 70–80% of cases, while its static counterpart B "is much worse than many other competitors." Iyer et al. (2013) show the regime actually **flips which measure wins**: degree leads under simultaneous (initial) attack, betweenness under sequential (recalculated) attack. Almeira et al. (2020), on ER networks, report that initial-attack critical exponents match random percolation while recalculated attacks belong to different universality classes, with recalculated betweenness producing an abrupt, explosive-percolation-like transition; secondary reporting of that paper puts the initial-vs-recalculated degree threshold gap at ≈0.12 — **four times your entire empirical random-minus-degree gap of 0.0305.** (I did not verify that number in the source; verify before printing it.)

**So the honest statement is: your targeted thresholds (0.169 empirical degree; 0.2023 and 0.1889 synthetic) are upper bounds on the true worst-case threshold, and the shortfall may be substantial.**

**The two things you get in return, which you should state:**

1. **Computational necessity, with a citation.** Wandelt et al. (2018) report betweenness at O(nm) per iteration, roughly cubic overall. At a median N of 15,404 nodes, 100 leaves, and 2,400 trials per leaf per mode, full recalculation is not merely expensive but infeasible. This is a legitimate, citable justification — not an excuse.
2. **Universality-class coherence.** Almeira et al. (2020) find initial attacks sit in the random-percolation universality class while recalculated attacks do not. Since your headline comparison is *random vs targeted p_c on a common footing*, staying entirely within one universality class is methodologically defensible and arguably preferable for the specific comparison you are making. Recalculated attacks would not be directly comparable to your random baseline in the same way.

> *Defensible sentence form:* "All targeted removals use centralities computed once on the intact network. Recalculating centralities after each removal is known to increase damage further (Holme et al., 2002), and iterative betweenness is the strongest known dismantling heuristic (Wandelt et al., 2018); our targeted thresholds are therefore upper bounds on the worst case rather than estimates of it. Recalculation is computationally prohibitive at our scale — betweenness is O(nm) per step on networks of median N ≈ 15,400 across 2,400 trials per leaf per mode — and static attacks have the compensating property of remaining in the random-percolation universality class (Almeira et al., 2020), keeping them directly commensurable with our random baseline."

**Recommended robustness check if resources permit:** run recalculated-betweenness removal on the **synthetic N = 1023 reference only**, where it is cheap, and report the shift. One number closes this entire line of criticism. If the shift is small on a network of that architecture, say so; if it is large, you have quantified your own bound and are strictly better off than leaving it unaddressed.

### Q4 — Localised vs random removal as the model of haustorial damage

**Short answer: the literature is on your side, more decisively than you may expect — but only because your system is a *single* network. Say so explicitly, because a reviewer citing Berezin et al. (2015) from memory will assume the opposite.**

**The case that random removal is an adequate — indeed conservative — proxy:**

1. **Shao et al. (2015):** for ER networks, localized attack is *exactly equivalent* to random attack (p_c = 1/⟨k⟩ in both). For random-regular networks with k₀ > 2, the localized-attack threshold is always *smaller* than the random-attack threshold — the network is **more** resilient to localized attack. The direction is governed by degree heterogeneity: only scale-free networks below a critical exponent λ_c are more vulnerable to localized attack. **Your networks (SD 0.848) are at the homogeneous, random-regular-like end, where the literature predicts localized damage is no worse than, and probably milder than, random damage at equal removed fraction.**

2. **Berezin et al. (2015)** — and this is the crucial one — is the paper that established the catastrophic localized-attack result, and it states plainly that this holds "in marked contrast to single networks," and that **in a single spatially embedded network a hole of any finite size cut in the lattice has no effect on overall system robustness.** The finite critical attack radius, constant at ≈300 nodes independent of system size (their Fig. 1d), arises *only* for two spatially embedded networks coupled by length-limited dependency links. **Your system has no second interdependent layer and no dependency coupling.** The mechanism that would make a localized haustorial lesion catastrophic is structurally absent.

3. **The intuition is simply correct here:** a compact lesion in a planar network is a hole. The giant component routes around it. Random removal at the same fraction scatters damage across the whole lamina and severs far more independent paths. Random removal is therefore the *more* damaging abstraction at equal removed fraction, making your p_c of 0.200 conservative rather than optimistic.

> *Defensible sentence form:* "A haustorium is a spatially contiguous disturbance, and localized attacks are known to be catastrophic in some spatially embedded systems (Berezin et al., 2015). That result, however, requires two spatially embedded networks coupled by length-limited dependency links; the same work reports that in a *single* spatially embedded network a finite localized hole does not propagate and leaves global connectivity essentially unaffected. Our system is a single planar network with no interdependent layer. Moreover, for networks with homogeneous degree distributions — the regime our leaves occupy, with degree SD 0.848 about a mean of 2.520 — localized removal is analytically no more damaging than random removal, and for regular-degree networks strictly less so (Shao et al., 2015). Random removal at a given removed fraction is therefore the more conservative abstraction, and our thresholds should be read as such."

**Where I must be honest that this defence is incomplete — and it is the real residual risk:**

- Shao et al.'s locality is defined on the **graph**, not in the **plane**. Extending their result to a genuinely 2D-embedded venation network is an inference, not a demonstrated theorem. Berezin et al.'s single-network statement *is* about a true lattice and does cover this, but a leaf is not a lattice: it is sparser (⟨k⟩ 2.52 vs 4) and 22.4% of its edges are bridges.
- **The one lesion that would break the argument is a lesion on the hierarchy, not on the lamina.** A haustorium on a midrib or a major secondary vein sits on the small set of high-betweenness bridge segments whose removal your own betweenness result shows is disproportionately damaging. In that specific case the localized attack *is* a betweenness attack, and your two results converge rather than conflict. This is not a weakness — **it is your best synthesis paragraph**, and it should be written explicitly: *the biologically realistic worst case is a proximal, hierarchy-sited haustorium, and that case is exactly what our betweenness mode bounds.* This closes the loop between Q1 and Q4 and gives the targeted modes a concrete biological referent without ever claiming the parasite computes anything.
- **A cheap, cited robustness check exists and I recommend running it.** Mander and Williams (2024) implemented precisely this on real venation networks: circular holes of fixed area, randomly positioned, 250 trials per taxon. Reproducing that as a fourth mode — remove a contiguous disc of nodes around a randomly chosen seed, sweep disc size — on even a subset of leaves would let you report a localized p_c alongside the random one, with a direct methodological precedent in the leaf-venation literature. If it comes out at or above 0.200, the objection is empirically dead in your own data.

## COUNTEREVIDENCE AND LIMITS

**1. Iyer et al. (2013) directly contradicts your betweenness > degree ordering for *initial-ranking* attacks.** Under simultaneous (initial-ranking) attack, degree was the most effective measure across most of their network classes — scale-free, exponential, clustered, disassortative. Betweenness only took the lead under sequential (recalculated) attack. Your attacks are initial-ranking, and you find betweenness more damaging. Almeira et al. (2020) is reported to concur that on small ER networks initial degree slightly outperforms initial betweenness, with the ordering reverting only under recalculation. **Your defence is that neither study includes a planar, narrow-degree network class — Iyer et al. say so explicitly — and Mou and Hayashi (2025) recover your ordering on planar proximity graphs. But their betweenness attack is recalculated, so this is not a clean match either.** I found **no** study that directly demonstrates initial-betweenness beating initial-degree on a planar graph with ⟨k⟩ ≈ 2.5. Your result may be the first; frame it as such, with the tie-degeneracy mechanism as the explanation, and do not overclaim literature support.

**2. The scale-free attack-tolerance canon may simply not apply to your networks, and this cuts against invoking it at all.** Albert et al. (2000) is explicit that robust-yet-fragile behaviour is displayed *only* by inhomogeneously wired networks. With degree SD 0.848 you are not in that class. Callaway et al. (2000), Holme et al. (2002), Motter and Lai (2002) and Iyer et al. (2013) all work primarily on non-spatial, locally tree-like configuration-model or scale-free graphs whose assumptions a planar embedding violates. **The paper should state plainly that it invokes this canon for framing and contrast, not for prediction.** Overreliance on it is a legitimate reviewer criticism.

**3. Are degree and betweenness meaningful proxies for *biological* vulnerability at all? There is real reason for doubt, and it should be conceded.** My searches surfaced findings in the road-network literature that critical nodes could not be identified from betweenness alone, and that highly central nodes important for flow transmission were not necessarily nodes whose loss harmed connectivity. In a hydraulic network the biologically meaningful failure quantity is not a shortest-path count but the flux and tension a conduit sustains under a Kirchhoff solve — a quantity your pipeline actually computes and betweenness only approximates. Shortest-path betweenness assumes flow follows geodesics; current-flow (random-walk) betweenness is the correct analogue for a resistor network and can differ substantially. **Concede this, and consider reporting current-flow betweenness, or at minimum the rank correlation between shortest-path betweenness and your own solved node fluxes.** Degree fares worse still: in a venation network, degree is very nearly a categorical variable distinguishing tips from branch points and carries almost no hydraulic meaning at all.

**4. Your degree-targeted attack is probably massively tie-degenerate, and its result may depend on the tie-breaking rule.** With ⟨k⟩ = 2.520 and SD 0.848 in a planar graph, the great majority of non-tip nodes share the modal degree. Removing the top ~20% by degree is then largely an arbitrary tie-broken selection among equivalent nodes. This is consistent with the small 15.7% reduction you observe, and it is the most likely true explanation of your betweenness > degree ordering. **A reviewer will find this. Report the tie counts and the tie-breaking rule yourself, and show that results are stable across randomised tie-breaks** — otherwise the degree mode is not clearly reproducible.

**5. Static rankings may understate targeted vulnerability by an amount larger than your headline effect.** Wandelt et al. (2018) find static betweenness "much worse than many other competitors" while iterative betweenness leads the field; the reported ER initial-vs-recalculated degree gap of ≈0.12 is four times your empirical median per-leaf gap of 0.0305. The random-vs-targeted separation you report is therefore not a robust estimate of the true worst case, and should not be presented as one.

**6. Betweenness centrality on the *synthetic reference only* limits the generality of your headline betweenness result.** The most interesting finding — that the backbone attack beats the hub attack — rests on a single N = 1023 mesh, not on the 100-leaf empirical cohort. The correlations that would establish it as a general property of real venation architecture (loop fraction, bridge fraction, mean degree vs the betweenness gap) cannot be computed. Any claim of generality is currently unsupported by your own data.

**7. The absence of cascade dynamics is a real structural limitation.** Motter and Lai (2002) show that load redistribution after node loss can cascade. Your removal is instantaneous and non-propagating, so hydraulic failures that would in reality redistribute tension onto surviving conduits and trigger further embolism are not modelled. This means the model may *understate* collapse severity in a direction the current framing does not acknowledge — worth one honest sentence.

**8. Mander and Williams (2024) is not numerically comparable to your results despite being the closest biological precedent.** Their damage is pixel-level, their metric is fractional connectivity to structural veins, and their retention thresholds (≈0.80, ≈0.60) are **not** commensurable with your node-removal p_c of 0.200. Do not tabulate them together.

**9. Bridge fraction, loop fraction and mean degree are not independent.** In a planar graph they are near-algebraically linked (loop fraction ≈ 1 − bridge fraction; both are tightly constrained by ⟨k⟩ via Euler's formula). Your correlations of +0.818 (loop vs targeted p_c), −0.818 (bridge vs targeted p_c), −0.786 and −0.793 (loop and mean degree vs the attack gap) are of nearly identical magnitude, which is what one expects from near-collinear predictors. **These are not four independent findings; they are one finding measured four ways,** and presenting them as mutually corroborating would be a genuine overstatement.

- **To the hydraulics / xylem-embolism agent:** Motter and Lai (2002)'s betweenness-as-load identity is the hinge between the network arm and the hydraulic arm of the paper. - **To the venation-architecture / reticulation agent:** Mander and Williams (2024) independently confirms in real leaf venation that reticulation confers damage tolerance and that collapse is percolation-like, matching the direction of your loop-fraction correlations.
