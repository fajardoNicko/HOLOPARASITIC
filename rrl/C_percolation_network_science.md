## ANSWERS TO THE THREE QUESTIONS

### Q1 — The 50 %-of-N₀ criterion: precedent, defensibility, and how to phrase it

**Verdict: the quantity is defensible, but the *name* is not. Rename it and the objection disappears.**

There is real precedent, in three layers:

1. **In-domain, peer-reviewed, same organ.** Mander & Williams (2024, *RSOS*) locate a "collapse transition" in traced leaf venation networks at a fixed level of retained function (~0.05 of the network still connected to the structural vein) and report cumulative area-under-curve robustness. A fixed-fraction functional criterion is already the established practice for venation robustness.
2. **The dismantling / optimal-percolation literature.** Braunstein et al. (2016, *PNAS*) define dismantling as removal leaving components "of sub-extensive size"; Morone & Makse (2015, *Nature*, DOI 10.1038/nature14604) frame the same problem as finding a minimal node set to fragment the giant component. The stopping size in this literature is a modelling choice.
3. **Explicit integrated-robustness precedent.** Schneider et al. (2011, *PNAS*) argue for an integrated robustness measure over a single critical point; arXiv:2603.04838 names the "S = 0.5 crossing" estimator alongside susceptibility-peak and Molloy–Reed.

**But there is a genuine, unfixable-by-citation problem:** at a true critical point the giant component is fractal, so S/N → 0 as N → ∞. A criterion pinned at S/N = 0.5 therefore *cannot* be measuring the critical point — by construction it sits in the supercritical phase. Calling it p_c invites the reviewer to apply critical-point machinery (finite-size scaling, universality, Molloy–Reed comparison) that the quantity does not obey, which is precisely how the finite-size sweep in Q3 went wrong. **The symbol is doing the damage, not the definition.**

**Fix:** rename to **f₅₀** (or p₅₀) and reserve p_c / f_c exclusively for the Callaway-style vanishing threshold already reported as 0.524. Then add a criterion-invariance check: recompute at S/N = 0.25 and 0.10 and show that the qualitative conclusions (targeted < random in 100/100 leaves; ⟨k⟩ and loop fraction as leading predictors) survive. **Invariance of the ranking under criterion choice is a far stronger defence than any citation.**

**Defensible sentence form:**

> "We characterise network fragility by f₅₀, the removed-node fraction at which the largest connected component falls below half of its undamaged size with probability 0.50. f₅₀ is an operational robustness index rather than a thermodynamic critical point: because the giant component is fractal at criticality, any criterion defined at a fixed fraction of system size necessarily lies in the supercritical regime and is expected to be size-dependent. We adopt it because it is comparable across networks spanning three orders of magnitude in N, and because fixed-fraction functional criteria are established practice in venation-robustness (Mander & Williams, 2024) and network-dismantling (Braunstein et al., 2016) analyses, where integrated robustness indices are likewise preferred to single critical points (Schneider et al., 2011). We report the vanishing threshold f_c separately, and we verify in Supplementary [X] that all reported orderings and predictor rankings are unchanged when the criterion is set to 0.25 or 0.10."

### Q2 — What transfers from random-graph theory to planar networks, and what does not

**Transfers.** (i) The *qualitative* ordering that degree-targeted removal is more damaging than random removal (Albert et al., 2000; Cohen et al., 2001) — the paper's 100/100 result is consistent. (ii) The generating-function machinery as a *reference-ensemble* calculation. (iii) The giant component as a formal order parameter (Molloy & Reed, 1995; Callaway et al., 2000).

**Does not transfer.**

- **The quantitative Molloy–Reed threshold — demonstrably, with numbers.** For a 3-regular graph, κ = 3 gives a tree-like removal threshold of 0.500. Kownacki (2008) measures 0.2640 on planar Φ³ random graphs; Djordjevic et al. (1982) give 0.3038 on the honeycomb lattice. **Two independent planar 3-regular systems, both 1.6–1.9× less removal-tolerant than tree-like theory predicts.** *(Arithmetic mine, from verified published thresholds.)* Corollary the paper should state: f_c = 0.524 and MR = 0.531 are **upper bounds**, and their mutual agreement is an internal consistency check between two tree-like calculations, not a validation.
- **The scale-free "no threshold" result** (Cohen et al., 2000). Irrelevant — planarity bounds degree, ⟨k⟩ = 2.520 with a narrow distribution.
- **Mean-field critical exponents.** A planar embedding is not in the mean-field class. This is exactly why the observed β ≈ 1.48 matches neither 1.0 nor 0.14 and why withdrawing the universality claim was correct.
- **Hub-driven attack intuition.** With ⟨k⟩ = 2.520 there are effectively no hubs, which *predicts* the modest 0.200 → 0.169 targeted gain. Frame this as confirmation, not weakness.

**Supporting statements to cite:** Radicchi & Castellano (2016) — the tree-like ansatz "holds only to a limited extent, as real graphs are often characterized by high frequencies of short loops," with residual error from "loops longer than three edges" (the areole regime). Melnik et al. (2011) — tree-based theory works "as long as the mean intervertex distance is sufficiently small… close to the value in a random network," a precondition that fails for planar meshes with path lengths ~√N. Radicchi (2015) — all analytic predictors degrade as the true threshold grows and none is a good proxy for fragile networks. Bashan et al. (2013) — spatial embedding qualitatively alters percolation behaviour.

**Defensible sentence form:**

> "Classical random-graph robustness theory transfers to these networks only qualitatively. The ordering of random versus degree-targeted removal is reproduced, but the quantitative Molloy–Reed threshold is not applicable: it is derived under a locally tree-like assumption that planar venation meshes (median loop fraction 0.776) violate, and the residual error of tree-like theory on real graphs is driven by loops longer than three edges (Radicchi & Castellano, 2016) — the areole regime. The magnitude of the discrepancy is documented for exactly this graph class: for 3-regular graphs the tree-like removal threshold is 0.500, whereas measured site thresholds correspond to 0.264 on planar Φ³ random graphs (Kownacki, 2008) and 0.304 on the honeycomb lattice (Djordjevic et al., 1982). We therefore report f_c = 0.524 and the Molloy–Reed value 0.531 as an upper bound and an ensemble reference, not as a prediction, and we note that their mutual agreement reflects a shared tree-like assumption rather than independent confirmation. The condition under which tree-based theory is nonetheless accurate — a mean intervertex distance close to the random-graph value (Melnik et al., 2011) — is not met here, since path lengths in planar meshes grow as √N."

### Q3 — The finite-size drift: is "no claim" the right call?

**No — "no claim" is an over-correction, and it is itself a reviewer target.** Withdrawing the *universality class* and the *β exponent* was correct and should stand. Withdrawing *all* interpretation of a deliberately designed, five-size, 40,000-trial-per-size sweep leaves an unexplained result in the paper, and reviewers ask why an experiment was run and not interpreted.

**What the literature says is going on — three converging explanations:**

1. **The drift is a predicted property of the criterion, not of the physics.** Because f₅₀ is pinned at a fixed *fraction* of system size, it is not a critical-point observable and has no reason to converge. In the tree-like limit this is exact and elementary: for a one-dimensional chain (the ⟨k⟩ → 2, loop-fraction → 0 limit), diluting by any fixed fraction f > 0 leaves a largest surviving run of order log N, so the surviving fraction → 0 for every f > 0 and the 50 %-criterion threshold necessarily → 0 as N grows. *(This is the standard 1-D percolation result, p_c = 1, stated in Stauffer & Aharony Ch. 1 — I did not verify it verbatim in this session; verify before citing a page.)* The audited loop-fraction drift 0.692 → 0.561 is movement toward exactly that limit, and the observed monotone fall 0.2701 → 0.0772 is what that mechanism produces. **This turns the drift from an embarrassment into an explained artefact.**
2. **Large finite-size effects are an acknowledged open feature** of network percolation (Dorogovtsev et al., 2008, abstract: "strong finite size effects"), and drifts strong enough to make prior conclusions "controversial" are documented (Mizutaka & Hasegawa, 2022).
3. **There may be no single transition to find.** Hébert-Dufresne & Allard (2019) show real networks can exhibit *smeared* or sequential transitions where order-parameter inhomogeneity does not vanish in the thermodynamic limit. Hierarchical vein orders are a strong structural candidate.

**Yes, there is a standard diagnostic you should have run.** Mizutaka & Hasegawa (2022) name it: finite-size scaling of the **peak of the second-largest cluster size** and of the **mean finite-cluster size (susceptibility)**, χ(p) = Σ s²n_s / Σ s·n_s with the giant component excluded, followed by a data collapse of S·N^(β/ν̄) against (p − p_c)·N^(1/ν̄). Newman & Ziff (2001) make this cheap — one O(N) union-find sweep yields the full p-curve, so the entire diagnostic costs far less than the 1e6-trial runs already performed. **Recommendation: run it before submission on the five pinned sizes.** Either the susceptibility-peak location converges (you recover a genuine critical point and a much stronger paper) or it does not (you have positive evidence for a smeared transition — also publishable, and citable to Hébert-Dufresne & Allard).

**Defensible sentence form (if the diagnostic is not run):**

> "Across a fixed-architecture sweep with box-counting dimension (1.384–1.391) and mean degree (2.487–2.493) held constant, f₅₀ fell monotonically from 0.2701 to 0.0772 as N increased from 1,023 to 16,383. A post hoc audit showed that loop fraction was not held constant, drifting from 0.692 to 0.561 over the same range, and loop fraction independently predicts f₅₀ (Spearman 0.727). We therefore make no claim of convergence or divergence. We note that this drift is anticipated on two independent grounds: f₅₀ is defined at a fixed fraction of system size and is consequently not a critical-point observable, and the loop-fraction drift moves the ensemble toward the tree-like limit, in which any fixed dilution leaves a largest component of sub-extensive size. Locating the pseudo-critical point by finite-size scaling of the susceptibility peak or of the second-largest cluster size (Mizutaka & Hasegawa, 2022), computed efficiently by the Newman–Ziff algorithm (Newman & Ziff, 2001), is the appropriate diagnostic and is left to future work."

**Better still, if run:** report the susceptibility-peak location alongside f₅₀ at all five sizes. This single addition converts the paper's most exposed section into its most rigorous one.

## COUNTEREVIDENCE AND METHODOLOGICAL RISK

**(a) Is the giant component biologically meaningful here? — the most serious exposure, and it is internal.**
For xylem, connectivity is necessary but not sufficient; the functional quantity is hydraulic conductance *to the transpiring surface*. A component containing 50 % of nodes that is severed from the petiole is hydraulically dead. Both in-domain precedents anchor to a source: Mander & Williams (2024) measure "the proportion of the network that remains attached to the structural vein," and Loepfe et al. (2007) model conductance along vessel paths. This compounds the recorded code/prose mismatch that root/petiole attachment is not enforced in `percolation.py`. **Mitigation: report, at minimum for a subsample, the fraction of nodes remaining connected to the petiole node alongside the GCC fraction. If the two curves track closely, one sentence closes the objection permanently. If they diverge, better to find out now.** A second, softer version: the paper solves a Hagen–Poiseuille/Kirchhoff resistor network, so the natural order parameter is *retained conductance*, not node count — a reviewer will ask why the connectivity abstraction was used when the conductance solver was already built.

**(b) Is an apparent threshold in a finite planar graph a genuine phase transition?**
Strictly, no. Singularities exist only in the thermodynamic limit; finite systems show smooth crossovers, and a well-defined transition point in a finite system "is not a true critical one." With a single leaf's crop window and no size sequence at fixed architecture that survived audit, the paper has no evidence of a genuine transition — only of a reproducible crossover. Worse, Hébert-Dufresne & Allard (2019) show that structurally heterogeneous real networks can exhibit *smeared* transitions in which inhomogeneity does not vanish in the thermodynamic limit; hierarchical vein orders make venation a prime candidate. **Mitigation: say "collapse" or "crossover," never "phase transition," unless the susceptibility-peak diagnostic is run. Withdrawing β and the universality class was correct; extend that discipline to the word "transition."**

**(c) Threshold estimates are demonstrably criterion-dependent.**
On one Barabási–Albert network (N = 2,000, m = 2), three standard estimators give 0.174 (susceptibility peak), 0.331 (S = 0.5 crossing), and 0.092 (Molloy–Reed) — a ~3.5× spread (arXiv:2603.04838; **preprint, not peer-reviewed — verify before citing**). Independently and peer-reviewed: Radicchi (2015) finds that across 109 real networks, no analytic indicator is "a good proxy for robustness of extremely fragile networks," and all degrade as the true threshold grows — the paper's regime exactly. Newman & Ziff (2001) document spanning probability and giant-component size as distinct observables. **The absolute value of f₅₀ is not comparable to any externally reported p_c computed under a different criterion, and the paper must say so explicitly rather than let a reviewer say it.**

**(d) Site vs bond is a modelling choice with consequences, and site may be the weaker one.**
Haustorial sinks and embolism act on *conduits* (edges), not on junctions. Loepfe et al. (2007) model vessel-level spread. Radicchi & Castellano (2016) report that their loop-correction improves site percolation substantially but bond percolation "much less apparent" — the site/bond distinction is not cosmetic in the presence of loops. Radicchi & Castellano (2015, *Nat. Commun.*) show site and bond percolation can even differ in critical exponents, **though only demonstrated for null-threshold scale-free graphs, so scope this claim carefully.** **Mitigation: justify site percolation explicitly on the grounds that junction-level sink imposition matches the haustorial connection geometry, and if feasible show that bond percolation reproduces the same predictor rankings on a subsample.**

**(e) f_c = 0.524 vs MR = 0.531 is not an independent validation.** Both are tree-like generating-function calculations on the same degree sequence. If the 1.6–1.9× planar overestimation documented for 3-regular graphs (Kownacki 2008; Djordjevic et al. 1982) carries over, the true planar vanishing fraction would be nearer 0.28–0.32 — which is at least consistent with the simulated f₅₀ = 0.200 lying below it. *(That last chain is my inference from verified inputs; present it as a plausibility check, not a result.)*

**(f) Reference hygiene.** Albert, Jeong & Barabási (2000) carries a published erratum (*Nature* 409:542, DOI 10.1038/35054111) correcting the exponential-network error-tolerance curves. Cohen et al. (2001) has a published Comment (arXiv:cond-mat/0109083). Cite the erratum; a reviewer who spots an uncorrected citation will assume the references were not opened.
