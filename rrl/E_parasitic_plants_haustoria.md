## THE STRUCTURAL-REMOVAL QUESTION

**Honest answer: there is real but narrow, indirect, and system-specific evidence — and it points to the host, not the parasite, as the agent of occlusion. There is essentially no evidence that holoparasitic attachment removes host vein junctions in a leaf.**

**What the evidence does support:**

- **Pérez-de-Luque et al. (2005)** is the strongest positive finding: in *Orobanche crenata*–legume interactions, an Alcian-blue-staining **carbohydrate substance of host origin fills host xylem vessel elements**, including vessels at a distance from the haustorium, and **host pericycle and endodermal cells lignify**, sealing the route into the vascular cylinder. This is a genuine loss of host conducting pathway.
- **Cao et al. (2023)** independently found **callose deposited in the host endodermis** and thickened host xylem cell walls in resistant melon under *Phelipanche aegyptiaca*.
- Both are **host defence responses**, are most pronounced in **resistant genotypes**, and are described as starving the *parasite*, not as harming the host.

**What the evidence contradicts:**

- **Echevarría-Zomeño et al. (2006)** — the same research group, different system — found callose "scarce" and explicitly **not implicated** in reinforcement in resistant sunflower against *O. cumana*, expressly distinguishing it from the *O. crenata*/legume case. So vessel occlusion is **not general**.
- **Jhu et al. (2022)** and **Kaiser et al. (2015)**, covering the *Cuscuta*–tomato system your paper is anchored to, both find that host defence is **cortical and hypodermal lignification/suberisation that arrests the parasite before it reaches the vascular cylinder**. Host vascular tissue is not occluded. In *susceptible* hosts — the case your model represents — the haustorium simply connects, and **no host vascular occlusion is reported at all**.
- **Auriac et al. (2024)** (title/record-level) report that broomrape penetration of host root tissue is **intracellular** — passage through host cells rather than excision of tissue.
- **Cochavi (2024)** reports that root hydraulic conductivity in parasitized sunflower **increases**.

**Two further problems you should not leave implicit:**

- **Organ mismatch.** Balios et al. (2025) state *Cuscuta*'s preferred infection sites are host **stems, petioles and pedicels**; *Orobanche*/*Phelipanche* attach to **roots**. Your network is a traced **leaf-lamina** venation crop window (Matos et al. 2024). **No holoparasite in your citation set routinely attaches haustoria to leaf-blade vein junctions.** A haustorial node inside a leaf lamina is a modelling device, and should be declared as one.
- **The one system where attachment genuinely reorganises host vasculature is the endoparasites**, not *Cuscuta*. In Apodanthaceae, the endophytic system grows through the host cambial zone and between secondary phloem cells, and in parasitized *Adesmia*, the cambium shifts to producing xylem parenchyma with **limited vessel production and halted fibre formation**. That is real structural alteration of host conducting tissue — but it is a **cambial developmental redirection in woody stems over years**, not junction deletion in a leaf, and it applies to a group (~1.6% of parasitic angiosperms) far outside your model system.

### Most defensible reframing of the node-removal abstraction

**Do not claim the haustorium excises vein junctions. It does not.** Three reframings, in descending order of defensibility:

**(A) — Recommended. Node removal is a hydraulic-functionality abstraction, not an anatomical one.** A vein junction is "removed" when the conduits meeting there cease to conduct — whether air-filled by cavitation, gel/carbohydrate-filled, callose-sealed, or lignin-walled. Nothing is physically excised in any of these cases; the network simply loses that path. This is standard in xylem network percolation and requires no parasite-specific warrant at all. State it plainly in Methods: *site removal represents loss of conducting function at a junction, from any cause, not physical destruction of tissue.*

**(B) — The strongest parasite-specific warrant, and the one that fits your alternative title.** The occlusion evidence you have (Pérez-de-Luque et al. 2005; Cao et al. 2023) shows that the mechanism removing host conductance is **the host's own defence response** — lignification of pericycle and endodermis, callose in the endodermis, carbohydrate deposits filling host vessel elements including vessels beyond the attachment point. Node removal therefore models **host-initiated vascular sealing at and around infection sites**, not parasite excision. This is a considerably more interesting story than the one currently framed, and it makes your second title ("Vein Reticulation as a Host-Resistance Trait") mechanistically coherent: *a highly reticulate network can afford to seal off more of itself before it fragments; a poorly reticulate one cannot.* Resistance then carries a topological cost, and reticulation buys the host the right to defend itself. This also explains, rather than excuses, why your calibrated sink alone embolises zero vessels: the sink and the sealing are separate processes, which is precisely your decoupling conclusion.

**(C) — Fallback.** Present the percolation half as a **generic structural-vulnerability assay** — robustness of real traced venation topologies to random conduit loss from any agent (drought embolism, pathogen occlusion, herbivory, mechanical damage) — with the parasite contributing only the tension boundary condition. This is what your results actually license, since at the calibrated K_h no network collapses at any attachment density and a threshold appears only at 2–3× K_h.

**Whichever you choose, one sentence of the form "we are not claiming haustorial attachment physically deletes host vein junctions; site removal is an abstraction for loss of conducting function, and the empirically documented route to that loss is host-derived occlusion in resistant interactions" will pre-empt this reviewer objection.** Trying to assert direct excision will not survive review, because Jhu et al. (2022) is well known and says the opposite for your model genus.

## COUNTEREVIDENCE AND LIMITS

1. **Broomrape host physiology largely does not degrade before emergence.** Cochavi (2024, *Planta* 261:4) states that detrimental effects "become apparent only after the emergence of the parasite's inflorescence," and that below-ground effects are minor. Cochavi et al. (2017) measured this directly: A, gs and E were **statistically indistinguishable** from controls throughout the below-ground phase. This is a direct challenge to "pre-symptomatic vascular collapse."

2. **Host root hydraulic conductivity goes UP, not down.** Cochavi (2024) reports that root hydraulic conductivity in parasitized sunflower is **increased**, explicitly contrasted with drought, which reduces vessel conductivity. Hosts respond to a parasitic sink by facilitating *more* flow, not by losing conductance — the opposite of a collapse trajectory.

3. **Hosts also compensate photosynthetically.** Cochavi (2024): carbon assimilation is similar in youngest and oldest leaves of infected and control plants; **intermediate leaves of infected plants show *higher* photosynthetic activity** and higher Rubisco. Sink-stimulated photosynthesis is a well-established feature of parasitic associations (Jeschke's *Cuscuta reflexa*–*Ricinus* work). The host is not passively draining.

4. **A holoparasite can impose a fitness cost with no water-status signature at all.** Ossa et al. (2021): *Tristerix aphyllus* reduced *Echinopsis chiloensis* fruit production by **16%** while stomatal traits showed **no difference** for parasitism and **δ¹³C and δ¹⁵N were unchanged** — δ¹³C being a time-integrated proxy for stomatal limitation. The authors conclude infection "does not have a profound effect on the physiology" of the host.

5. **The front-line mechanistic explanation is hormonal, not hydraulic.** Rodriguez & Albert (2026) attribute *Striga*'s sink strength to a **molecular escape from ABA regulation** via disruption of the tryptophan lock in ShPP2C1 — a signalling hijack. In *Cuscuta*/*Mikania*, host gs falls (Shen et al. 2007), the wrong direction for pure hydraulic drainage and consistent with ABA-mediated closure. Search-level literature reports host ABA rising at 13 and 33 DAP in that system. Jiang et al. (2003) themselves found parasite leaf ABA **53×** that of the host with stomata nonetheless open — the sink is under active hormonal control.

6. **The damage that matters agronomically is nutritional and carbon-based.** Macronutrient application reduced Striga-induced grain loss in tolerant sorghum genotypes from **80% to 5%** (*Annals of Botany* 134:59, 2024, search-level — trace before citing). González de Andrés et al. (2024) nominate **potassium depletion and manganese accumulation** as the earliest warning signals of host decline, ahead of hydraulic indicators. Jeschke et al. (1994) quantified holoparasite draw as **C and N mass flux** because that is the currency that matters.

7. **Host responses are frequently systemic or whole-plant, not local-vascular.** Documented systemic effects include altered root:shoot allocation, whole-canopy leaf-area loss (88% in Shen et al. 2007), reduced specific leaf area from mesophyll loss, and complete inhibition of flowering. Ortiz-Bustos et al. (2017) detected the earliest signal in **leaf carotenoid and fluorescence** properties of a shoot far from a root infection.

8. **Vessel occlusion is not general and belongs to resistant genotypes.** Echevarría-Zomeño et al. (2006) found callose "scarce" and **not implicated** in resistant sunflower, explicitly distinguishing their system from *O. crenata*/legume. Jhu et al. (2022) and Kaiser et al. (2015) locate *Cuscuta* host defences in the **cortex and hypodermis, outside the vascular cylinder**. In compatible interactions — the case your model represents — no host vascular occlusion is reported.

9. **Penetration is non-destructive.** Auriac et al. (2024) report broomrape penetration of sunflower root tissue is **intracellular** (record-level; I could not read the paper).

10. **Organ mismatch, restated as a limit.** *Cuscuta* preferentially infects **stems, petioles and pedicels** (Balios et al. 2025); *Orobanche*/*Phelipanche* infect **roots**. Your traced networks are **leaf-lamina crop windows**. Neither the haustorial attachment nodes nor the occlusion sites documented in the literature are located in a leaf blade.

11. **Holoparasites may be weaker hydraulic sinks than hemiparasites.** *Cuscuta*'s near-absence of functional stomata (Balios et al. 2025) removes the transpirational engine that drives the 20% diversion in *Rhinanthus* and the 6–40× ratio in *Striga*. Treating a hemiparasite figure as a floor for a holoparasite is the reverse of conservative.

12. **Even the best-measured hydraulic case is modest and does not collapse.** Griebel et al. (2022) is your ally on the sink but also a limit on the collapse narrative: 0.6 MPa depression, +11 pts modelled PLC, and hydraulic *risk* elevated — not hydraulic *failure*, and embolism was never actually measured.
